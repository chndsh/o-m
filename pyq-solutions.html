1. Questions on Organization

Q1: Define Organization as a social system. What role does an organization play in the professional growth of an employee? What is the need for an informal organization?

  • Organization as a Social System:
  • An organization is a structured social unit of people systematically arranged and cooperating together to achieve defined collective objectives in a dynamic environment (Amitai Etzioni; Keith Davis).
  • It operates as a socio-technical system: integrates human beings (social/psychological system) with tools, workflows, and structures (technical system) to transform inputs into valuable societal outputs.
  • Role in Professional Growth of an Employee:
  • Skill & Competence Development: Provides structured training programs, challenging assignments, and on-the-job exposure.
  • Career Advancement: Establishes clear promotion pathways, hierarchical ladders, and merit-based rewards.
  • Mentorship & Knowledge Transfer: Facilitates interaction with senior leaders and cross-functional technical specialists.
  • Professional Identity & Networking: Bestows organizational status, professional recognition, and collaborative networks.
  • Need for an Informal Organization:
  • Psychological & Social Satisfaction: Satisfies belongingness, companionship, and social affiliation needs that rigid formal hierarchies ignore.
  • Speed of Communication: The informal "grapevine" transmits urgent messages and peer feedback far faster than formal scalar chains.
  • Stress & Grievance Buffer: Provides an emotional safety valve for employees to discuss work pressures and anxieties informally.
  • Fosters Team Solidarity: Enhances group cohesion, mutual assistance during workload spikes, and job satisfaction.

Q2: Define Organization as an Open System. Why is an organization considered an open system?

  • Open System Definition: A set of interrelated and interdependent subsystems that dynamically interacts with, imports energy/resources from, and exports products/services to its external environment.
  • Why Organizations are Open Systems:
  • Resource Importation (Inputs): Draws raw materials, human labor, financial capital, and information continuously from the environment.
  • Transformation Process: Converts inputs through technical, operational, and managerial processes (Men-Machine systems).
  • Exportation (Outputs): Releases finished goods, services, wastes, and information back into society and market.
  • Feedback Loops & Homeostasis: Monitors environmental signals (customer demands, competitor pricing, regulations) to adjust internal operations.
  • Entropy Resistance & Adaptation: Constantly adapts to shifting market conditions to survive; closed systems suffer entropy (decay and death).

Q3: Explain the Principles of Organization (Koontz & Weihrich).

  1. Principle of Unity of Goals: Departmental and individual efforts must align without contradiction toward corporate objectives.
  2. Principle of Span of Control: The number of direct subordinates a manager can effectively supervise is limited (typically 4–8 at upper echelons, 8–15 at lower levels).
  3. Scalar Principle (Chain of Command): An unbroken line of authority must run from the highest executive to the lowest operative.
  4. Principle of Unity of Command: Each employee must report to and receive direct orders from only one superior.
  5. Principle of Delegation of Authority: Authority delegated to a subordinate must be adequate to accomplish assigned responsibilities.
  6. Principle of Parity of Authority & Responsibility: Authority must equal responsibility (Authority = Responsibility).
  7. Principle of Absoluteness of Responsibility: A superior cannot escape ultimate accountability for the actions and performance of subordinates.
  8. Principle of Departmentation: Organizational activities must be systematically classified into logical functional or product units.
  9. Principle of Balance: Optimum structural balance must be maintained between centralized control and decentralized autonomy.
  10. Principle of Flexibility: Organizational structures must adapt smoothly to technological, competitive, and legal shifts without collapse.
  11. Principle of Separation: Evaluating personnel should not report to the operational department whose performance they are evaluating.
  12. Principle of Leadership Facilitation: The structure must foster initiative, independent decision-making, and executive talent growth.

Q5: Explain Organizational Behavior as a Multidisciplinary Field.

Organizational Behavior (OB) investigates the impact of individuals, groups, and structures on behavior within organizations. It synthesizes insights from: 1. Psychology: Contributes principles of learning, motivation, personality, perception, training, leadership effectiveness, and job stress. 2. Sociology: Analyzes group dynamics, organizational culture, formal structure, communication networks, power, and conflict. 3. Social Psychology: Investigates behavioral change, attitude measurement, communication flows, trust building, and group decision processes. 4. Anthropology: Explores organizational culture, comparative values, attitudes, and cross-cultural business environments. 5. Political Science: Studies power acquisition, coalition formation, corporate politicking, and conflict allocation.

2. Questions on Management

Q1: Define Management. Explain its Core Functions.

  • Definition: Management is the process of planning, organizing, staffing, leading, and controlling available resources (Men, Money, Material, Method, Machine) to accomplish predetermined objectives efficiently and effectively.
  • Five Core Functions:
  • Planning: Deciding in advance what to do, how to do it, when to do it, and who is to do it; establishes goals and selects optimal action paths.
  • Organizing: Grouping activities into departments, assigning responsibilities, establishing authority relationships, and allocating physical resources.
  • Staffing: Determining manpower needs, recruiting, selecting, placing, training, appraising, and compensating qualified personnel.
  • Directing / Leading: Guiding, stimulating, supervising, and motivating employees to channel their energies enthusiastically toward enterprise goals.
  • Controlling: Measuring actual operational performance against planned standards, identifying deviations, and executing corrective actions.

Q2: "Management is both a Science and an Art." Discuss with examples.

  • Management as a Science:
  • Possesses a systematized body of knowledge, concepts, principles, and theories developed through empirical observation and experimentation (e.g., Taylor's time-motion studies, Fayol's 14 principles).
  • Utilizes scientific methods, mathematical models, and cause-and-effect relationships (e.g., inventory EOQ formulas, PERT/CPM scheduling).
  • Management as an Art:
  • Involves the practical, creative, and personalized application of theoretical knowledge to solve real-world problems.
  • Requires intuition, emotional intelligence, personal judgment, and subjective leadership skills (e.g., motivating a demoralized team, negotiating a labor contract dispute).
  • Conclusion: Science provides the underlying knowledge base and principles; Art provides the personalized skill, intuition, and practice to apply those principles successfully. Hence, management is an integrated synthesis of both.

  • Conceptual Skills: Cognitive ability to visualize the organization as a unified whole, comprehend subsystem interdependence, and formulate strategic vision; essential for top executives.

Q4: List and Explain the Models of Management.

  1. Hierarchical Model: Emphasizes top-down formal authority and downward delegation; structural clarity, but ignores horizontal communication and charisma.
  2. Task-Oriented Model: Organizes managerial work chronologically around core activities: Planning $ o$ Organizing $ o$ Directing $ o$ Controlling.
  3. Allocation Model: Focuses on acquiring, allocating, monitoring, and replenishing the enterprise's resource stocks (5Ms).
  4. Transactional Model: Treats management as a crucial value-adding stage in the operational chain transforming raw inputs into finished products.
  5. Team Effort Model: Views the manager as an empowering team coach orchestrating interlocking groups via trust and mutual collaboration.
  6. Knowledge-Oriented Model: Focuses on accumulating organizational knowledge and systematically diffusing expertise through mentoring and coaching.
  7. Goal-Concentrated Model: Translates strategic enterprise goals into tactical milestones using continuous internal/external feedback loops.

Q3: Explain Taylor's Scientific Management Theory: Principles, Tools, Contributions, and Limitations.

  • Four Principles of Scientific Management:
  • Develop a science for each element of a job (replace rule-of-thumb guesswork with time-motion studies).
  • Scientifically select, train, and develop workers (match physical/mental traits to tasks).
  • Hearty cooperation between management and workers (ensure work aligns with scientific methods).
  • Equal division of work and responsibility (management handles planning; workers handle execution).
  • Key Tools:
  • Functional Foremanship: 8 specialized bosses (Planning: Instruction Card Clerk, Route Clerk, Time & Cost Clerk, Disciplinarian; Execution: Gang Boss, Speed Boss, Repair Boss, Inspector).
  • Work Study: Time, motion, fatigue, and method studies.
  • Differential Piece-Rate System: Higher wage rate for workers hitting/exceeding standard; penalty rate for below-standard.
  • Contributions: Established scientific job standardization, mass production efficiency, and modern production scheduling.
  • Limitations: Dehumanized labor, caused monotony, generated worker anxiety over rate cuts, and violated unity of command.

Q4: Are Fayol's 14 Principles of Management Applicable in Today's Organizations?

  • Fayol's 14 Principles (DAD U C USSR O I SEE):
  • Division of Work, 2. Authority & Responsibility, 3. Discipline, 4. Unity of Command, 5. Centralization, 6. Unity of Direction, 7. Subordination of Individual Interest, 8. Scalar Chain, 9. Remuneration, 10. Order, 11. Equity, 12. Stability of Tenure, 13. Initiative, 14. Esprit de Corps.
  • Modern Applicability:
  • Still Highly Applicable: Division of work (specialization in tech), Equity (fair HR practices), Discipline, Initiative (agile autonomy), and Esprit de Corps (team culture).
  • Evolving / Adapted:
    • Unity of Command: Broken in matrix organizations where project engineers report to both functional managers and project managers.
    • Scalar Chain: Replaced by flat structures, Slack/email, and instant cross-communication networks (modern digital "Gang Planks").
    • Centralization: Shifts dynamically toward decentralized empowerment in knowledge industries.

Q5: Which Management Theory is Best Suited for Organizations in Nepal?

  • Recommendation: The Contingency (Situational) Management Theory.
  • Justification in Nepalese Context:
  • Diverse Business Landscape: Nepal features tiny cottage industries, state-owned public corporations, family-owned trading houses, and modern IT outsourcing firms; no single universal theory fits all.
  • Environmental Turbulence: Unpredictable political shifts, trade transit vulnerabilities, infrastructure hurdles, and brain drain require adaptive situational decision-making rather than rigid formulas.
  • Socio-Cultural Dynamics: Combines technical structure (Scientific/Administrative) with paternalistic, relationship-driven workplace culture (Behavioral/Human Relations) tailored to specific local conditions.

Q3: Explain Co-operative Societies: Principles and Types.

  • Concept & Guiding Principle: Voluntary association of individuals joining democratically to satisfy mutual economic needs; principle: "Each for all and all for each" (service motive over profit; "One member, one vote").
  • Types of Co-operatives:
  • Consumer Co-operatives: Purchase wholesale goods directly to eliminate retail markups for member consumers.
  • Producer Co-operatives: Supply raw materials, tooling, and machinery to small artisans and manufacturing members.
  • Marketing Co-operatives: Aggregate agricultural produce to bypass exploitative middlemen and secure fair market prices.
  • Credit Co-operatives (Saving & Credit): Provide affordable credit and encourage local savings to protect members from usurious moneylenders.
  • Housing Co-operatives: Acquire land and develop residential housing/plots for members at cost price.
  • Farming Co-operatives: Consolidate fragmented land parcels for mechanized, high-yield cooperative agriculture.

Q2: What are the Causes of Line and Staff Conflicts? How can they be Resolved?

  • Line Managers' Grievances against Staff:
  • Staff specialists are ivory-tower theorists lacking practical shop-floor realism.
  • Staff oversteps advisory bounds and tries to command line workers directly.
  • Staff claims credit for operational successes but blames line managers for failures.
  • Staff Specialists' Grievances against Line:
  • Line managers are conservative, resistant to change, and reject modern technical methods.
  • Line managers do not consult staff early enough during planning.
  • Staff lacks authority to implement its own well-researched recommendations.
  • Resolution:
  • Clearly demarcate line (command) versus staff (advisory) boundaries in job descriptions.
  • Educate line managers to leverage expert advice; instruct staff to sell ideas through persuasion, not orders.
  • Foster mutual consultation and joint accountability for project outcomes.

6. Questions on Purchasing and Marketing Management

Q1: State the Principles (5 Rights) and Functions of Purchasing Department.

  • 5 Rights of Purchasing:
  • Right Quality: Materials match required technical specifications and functional utility.
  • Right Quantity: Orders align with Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) to minimize carrying and stockout costs.
  • Right Price: Lowest total landed cost consistent with acceptable quality and delivery reliability.
  • Right Time: Deliveries synchronized with production master schedules to prevent idle factory downtime.
  • Right Source: Reliable vendors with sound financial stability, ethical practices, and technical capacity.
  • Functions of Purchasing Department:
  • Processing purchase requisitions from operational departments.
  • Market research, supplier evaluation, and vendor rating.
  • Floating tenders/RFQs, bid analysis, and commercial negotiations.
  • Issuing formal Purchase Orders (POs) and expediting deliveries.
  • Materials inspection (verifying quality/quantity) and clearing vendor invoices for payment.

Q2: Explain the Step-by-Step Purchasing Procedure.

  1. Requisition: Receipt of Purchase Requisition (PR) detailing items and quantities from using department.
  2. Vendor Identification: Exploring supply markets and identifying qualified prospective suppliers.
  3. Inquiry & Quotations: Floating Request for Quotation (RFQ) / Tender notice to vendors.
  4. Comparative Evaluation: Analyzing bids based on price, delivery timeline, payment terms, and quality warranty.
  5. Vendor Selection & PO Issuance: Awarding contract and issuing legally binding Purchase Order (PO).
  6. Expediting & Follow-Up: Monitoring vendor fabrication and transit progress to ensure timely arrival.
  7. Receiving & Inspection: Checking incoming consignments against PO specs via Goods Receipt Note (GRN).
  8. Invoice Approval & Payment: Matching PO, Delivery Challan, and Invoice ("3-Way Match") before finance payment.

Q3: Explain the Methods of Purchasing.

  1. Purchasing by Requirement (Hand-to-Mouth): Buying materials strictly as immediate needs emerge; minimizes inventory carrying cost, but risks stockouts and loses bulk discounts.
  2. Market Purchasing: Buying in advance during periods of favorable market price dips to capture cost savings.
  3. Speculative Purchasing: Purchasing quantities far beyond production needs to resell at profit when prices climb (pure financial speculation).
  4. Purchasing for a Specific Future Period: Buying routine materials required for a fixed operational timeframe (e.g., next 3–6 months).
  5. Contract Purchasing: Formal long-term supply agreement fixing price, delivery schedule, and quality benchmarks over extended periods.
  6. Scheduled Purchasing: Suppliers deliver predetermined quantities at precise intervals synchronized with manufacturing schedules.
  7. Group Purchasing of Small Items: Aggregating diverse small hardware/supplies with a single vendor to cut purchase order processing costs.
  8. Co-operative Purchasing: Small industrial or agricultural enterprises pooling demands to leverage volume discounts.

Q4: Explain the Functions of Marketing. What are the Challenges of Marketing Software Products in Nepal?

  • Functions of Marketing:
  • Exchange Functions: Buying, assembling, and selling.
  • Physical Distribution Functions: Transportation and storage/warehousing.
  • Facilitating Functions: Financing, risk bearing, quality standardization/grading, and market research.
  • Challenges of Marketing Software Products in Nepal:
  • Lack of International Payment Gateways: Inability to integrate seamless global merchant payments (Stripe/PayPal restrictions in Nepal).
  • Intellectual Property & Piracy Issues: Weak enforcement of software copyright laws leading to widespread unauthorized copying.
  • Client Price Sensitivity: Local enterprises often undervalue digital assets, demanding customized enterprise solutions at cheap commodity rates.
  • Brain Drain: Intense outward migration of top software developers to western markets complicates sustained product maintenance and customer support.
  • Low Digital Literacy & Resistance to Change: Traditional business sectors frequently resist transitioning from manual paperwork to automated SaaS tools.

Q4: Explain the Factors Governing Wage and Salary Structure. What are the 3 Layers of Wages?

  • Factors Governing Wage Structure:
  • Organization's financial ability to pay.
  • Demand and supply dynamics of specialized labor in the market.
  • Cost of living indices and inflation rates in the locality.
  • Prevailing wage rates across competing firms in the industry.
  • Trade union collective bargaining strength.
  • Job requirements, hazard levels, and technical complexity determined via job evaluation.
  • Three Layers of Wages:
  • Minimum Wage: Statutory wage floor providing bare survival subsistence (food, shelter, basic clothing) for workers living near the poverty line.
  • Fair Wage: Above minimum wage; ensures equal pay for equal work, reflecting regional capacity and industry productivity standards.
  • Living Wage: Highest standard; ensures health, decency, comfort, social security, children's education, and contingency protection.

Q5: What is Talent Poaching? How can HR Managers Counteract it?

  • Talent Poaching: The aggressive, deliberate practice of competitors recruiting an enterprise’s top-performing technical specialists or executives by offering inflated salaries and perks.
  • HR Countermeasures:
  • Competitive Retention Packages: Employee Stock Ownership Plans (ESOPs), retention bonuses, and profit sharing ("Golden Handcuffs").
  • Clear Career Ladders & Development: Transparent promotion trajectories and continuous high-value international training programs.
  • Empowering Organizational Culture: High psychological safety, technical autonomy, recognition, and healthy work-life balance.
  • Contractual Safeguards: Well-drafted non-compete clauses, non-solicitation covenants, and mandatory notice periods.

8. Questions on Motivation

Q1: Explain Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs. Why is it Called a Satisfaction-Progression Process?

  • Maslow's 5 Levels:
  • Physiological Needs (survival: food, salary) $ o$ 2. Safety/Security Needs (job tenure, insurance) $ o$ 3. Social/Belongingness Needs (friendship, team culture) $ o$ 4. Esteem Needs (recognition, status) $ o$ 5. Self-Actualization Needs (full creative potential).
  • Satisfaction-Progression Process:
  • Maslow posited that human needs emerge in a rigid sequential progression. An individual focuses on satisfying the lowest unmet deficiency need; once that need is substantially satisfied, it ceases to motivate, and the individual progresses upward to the next tier (Satisfaction activates the next higher need).

Q3: Explain the Irony of "Falling from Grace" while Ascending Maslow's Pyramid.

  • The Phenomenon:
  • As ambitious executives climb to the upper tiers of Maslow's pyramid (accumulating immense status, esteem, and unchecked autonomy), some experience a catastrophic moral collapse (committing financial fraud, corruption, embezzlement, or criminal offenses).
  • Underlying Causes:
  • Hubris & Invulnerability: Attaining high power creates cognitive distortions where leaders believe they are above company rules and legal frameworks.
  • Isolation & Lack of Accountability: Top executives become insulated from critical feedback, surrounded by sycophants who rubber-stamp unethical maneuvers.
  • Goal Displacement & Greed: Esteem needs morph into insatiable vanity and public posturing, driving reckless shortcuts to preserve elite status at all costs.

Q4: How does Maslow's Theory Explain Laxmi Prasad Devkota's Tireless Quest for Literary Masterpieces?

  • Context: The Great Poet (Mahakavi) Laxmi Prasad Devkota suffered severe lifelong financial poverty, physical illness, and deprivation (chronic failure of physiological and economic safety needs).
  • Maslow's Explanation:
  • While Maslow's baseline model assumes lower needs must be met first, Maslow acknowledged rare, exceptional creative geniuses whose Self-Actualization needs completely override basic physical needs.
  • Devkota was driven by an uncontrollable intrinsic creative impulse to produce immortal literary works (Muna Madan, Shakuntala, Sulochana). His artistic expression was the ultimate manifestation of self-actualization transcending physical deprivation.

Q5: Explain Vroom's VIE Expectancy Theory. Why is it Classified as a Process Theory?

  • The VIE Formula: $$ ext{Motivational Force (M)} = ext{Expectancy (E)} imes ext{Instrumentality (I)} imes ext{Valence (V)}$$
  • Expectancy ($E o P$): Probability belief that one's effort will achieve desired performance ($0.0 \le E \le 1.0$).
  • Instrumentality ($P o O$): Subjective probability that successful performance will be rewarded with a specific outcome ($0.0 \le I \le 1.0$).
  • Valence ($V$): Perceived value/desirability of the expected reward in the employee's eyes (Positive, Zero, or Negative).
  • Multiplicative Rule: If any single component ($E, I, ext{or } V$) equals zero, total motivational force collapses to zero.
  • Why it is a Process Theory:
  • Unlike content theories (Maslow, Herzberg) which merely list what specific needs motivate individuals, Vroom's theory analyzes the cognitive, psychological process and behavioral choices employees use to calculate effort based on perceived probabilities and rewards.

  • The Model: Evaluates leader behavior along two 9-point axes: Concern for Production (X-axis, 1–9) and Concern for People (Y-axis, 1–9).
  • Five Primary Combinations:
  • (1,1) Impoverished Management: Minimum effort; indifferent to both production and people; passive survival.
  • (9,1) Authority-Compliance / Task: Maximum focus on output; workers treated as human machinery; high short-term output, extreme turnover.

Q2: What Leadership Style is Most Appropriate for Modern Engineering Projects?

  • Answer: A Situational / Contingency Synthesis of Democratic and Directive Leadership.
  • Justification:
  • Design, Architecture, and Brainstorming Phases: Democratic / Participative style is mandatory. Engineering systems are complex; inviting technical debate from software, electrical, and mechanical specialists fosters innovation and catches subtle design bugs.
  • Execution, Safety, and Critical Deadlines: Directive / Task-oriented style is required during commissioning, safety emergency protocols, and hard client release deadlines where split-second decisive coordination is essential.

10. Questions on Entrepreneurship

Q1: Define Entrepreneur and Entrepreneurship. State the Functions of an Entrepreneur.

  • Definitions:
  • Entrepreneur: A visionary individual who takes financial, technical, and psychological risks to assemble land, labor, and capital to commercialize innovations and exploit market opportunities.
  • Entrepreneurship: The dynamic socio-economic process of creating incremental wealth through innovation, calculated risk-taking, and venture establishment.
  • Joseph Schumpeter's 5 Entrepreneurial Functions (Innovation Theory):
  • Introduction of a new product or new quality of a good.
  • Introduction of a new method of production.
  • Opening of a new, untapped market.
  • Conquest of a new supply source of raw materials.
  • Carrying out the new organization of an industry ("Creative Destruction").

Q2: Why Does a Developing Nation (like Nepal) Urgently Need to Promote Entrepreneurship?

  1. Employment Generation: Small and medium enterprises (SMEs) are labor-intensive, creating immediate jobs for youth and curbing devastating brain drain.
  2. Import Substitution & Trade Deficit Reduction: Local manufacturing replaces expensive foreign imports, preserving foreign currency reserves.
  3. Balanced Regional Development: Decentralizes industrial activity away from Kathmandu valley into rural districts.
  4. Mobilization of Domestic Resources: Channels idle local savings, indigenous craftsmanship, and domestic raw materials into productive economic activity.
  5. Poverty Alleviation: Distributes wealth equitably across rural communities through self-employment and micro-enterprises.

Q3: Explain the 7 Steps for Establishing a Small Scale Industrial Unit.

  1. Form of Ownership Selection: Choosing sole proprietorship, partnership, or private limited company based on liability, capital, and scale.
  2. Product Selection & Market Feasibility: Conducting market surveys to verify demand, competitive pricing, customer pain points, and technical viability.
  3. Location & Site Selection: Selecting site based on access to raw materials, cheap labor, transportation, reliable electrical power, and water.
  4. Financing Arrangement: Estimating fixed capital (plant, machinery) and working capital; securing bank term loans, equity, and government seed funds.
  5. Government Approvals & Statutory Licensing: Registering with Department of Industry / OCR, obtaining environmental clearance, PAN/VAT, and local ward licenses.
  6. Plant Machinery Procurement & Factory Layout: Purchasing production machinery, setting up ergonomic shop-floor layout, hiring staff, and conducting trial production runs.
  7. Marketing, Pricing, and Commercial Launch: Establishing distribution channels, setting cost-plus or market-penetration pricing, launching digital/print promotional campaigns, and commencing commercial sales.

Q4: "Silicon Valley is the Best Example of Successful Entrepreneurship." Discuss the Ecosystem Factors.

Silicon Valley represents the global benchmark of an entrepreneurial ecosystem due to: 1. Academic & Research Synergy: World-class research institutions (Stanford University, UC Berkeley) generating cutting-edge technological breakthroughs. 2. Venture Capital Concentration: Dense network of venture capitalists (Sand Hill Road) and angel investors willing to fund high-risk, high-reward innovations. 3. Culture of Tolerance for Failure: Failure is viewed as an honorable learning experience rather than a career-ending stigma. 4. Dense Talent Clustering & Network Effects: Unprecedented concentration of elite software engineers, serial founders, and patent attorneys creating rapid knowledge spillover.

11. Questions on Case Studies

Q1: Define Case Study and its Objectives.

  • Definition: An intensive, comprehensive empirical investigation of a real-world unit (an individual, company, crisis, or operational event) examined within its authentic business context.
  • Core Objectives:
  • Develop practical diagnostic problem-solving skills under resource limitations.
  • Train managers to distinguish underlying root problems from surface noise/symptoms.
  • Bridge the divide between abstract theoretical models and real executive decisions.
  • Practice articulating, debating, and defending strategic business alternatives.

Q2: Explain the 5 Phases and the 6 Steps of Conducting a Case Study.

  • 5 Phases of Case Study:
  • Exploration Phase: Initial reading of corporate history, timeline, characters, and environmental setting.
  • Diagnostic Phase: Identifying core problems and distinguishing symptoms from root causes.
  • Analysis Phase: Examining qualitative/quantitative facts, financial ratios, and testing hypotheses.
  • Alternative & Decision Phase: Generating feasible strategic alternatives, evaluating tradeoffs, and recommending the optimal path.
  • Synthesis & Generalized Learning Phase: Reflecting on the case to distill generalized management principles.
  • 6 Steps of Conducting a Case Study:
  • Selection of Case $ o$ 2. Collection of Evidence/Data $ o$ 3. Preliminary Analysis & Hypotheses $ o$ 4. Thorough Investigation $ o$ 5. Follow-Up Reliability Verification $ o$ 6. Generalization & Written Report.

Q3: Explain the 5Ws & 1H Analytical Framework for Solving Engineering Case Studies.

  • What: What is the exact problem, breakdown, or crisis occurring?
  • Why: Why did this failure occur? What were the root organizational, technical, or managerial causes?
  • When: When did the symptoms begin, and what is the urgency timeline for resolution?
  • Where: Where in the process, plant, or hierarchy is the bottleneck situated?
  • Who: Who are the responsible stakeholders, and who is impacted by potential remedies?
  • How: How can the optimal corrective action be implemented within time and budget constraints?

Q4: What are the Types and Limitations of Case Studies?

  • Types:
  • Illustrative: Describes real practice to validate existing management theory.
  • Exploratory (Pilot): Preliminary study to identify research questions before large-scale investigation.
  • Cumulative: Aggregates data from multiple sites/times to enable wider generalization.
  • Critical Instance: Examines a unique situation to question or disprove a universal assumption.
  • Situational: Investigates a specific isolated crisis (e.g., an industrial strike) from all stakeholder perspectives.
  • Limitations:
  • Lack of statistical generalizability to broad populations.
  • Vulnerability to researcher subjectivity and personal interpretation bias.
  • Inability to replicate exact real-world circumstances in controlled laboratory conditions.

13. High-Yield Short Notes & Conceptual Questions

1. Management by Objectives (MBO)

  • Proposed by Peter Drucker (1954) in The Practice of Management.
  • A collaborative managerial system where superior and subordinate jointly identify common goals, define individual areas of responsibility in terms of measurable expected results (KPIs), and use these metrics as guides for operating the unit and assessing performance.
  • Steps: 1. Set organizational goals $ o$ 2. Establish departmental objectives $ o$ 3. Jointly agree on individual milestones $ o$ 4. Monitor ongoing progress $ o$ 5. Conduct performance appraisal and rewards.

2. Collective Bargaining & Industrial Relations

  • Collective Bargaining: The formal negotiation process between an organized trade union representing workers and management representatives to determine fair employment terms (wages, working hours, safety protocols, benefits, grievance procedures).
  • Industrial Relations: The overall dynamic relationship between management, employees/unions, and the state governing employment contracts, industrial peace, conflict resolution, and labor welfare.

3. Span of Control: Tall vs Flat Structure

  • Span of Control: The number of direct subordinates an executive can supervise effectively.
  • Narrow Span: Leads to a Tall Structure with many hierarchical tiers; provides close supervision and detailed control, but creates communication delays, red tape, and high managerial payroll costs.
  • Wide Span: Leads to a Flat Structure with few hierarchical tiers; encourages delegation, fast communication, and lower overhead, but risks manager overload and loose supervision.

4. Computer Integrated Manufacturing (CIM)

  • The total automation and integration of all factory production and engineering processes through computer networks and database systems.
  • Integrates Computer-Aided Design (CAD), Computer-Aided Manufacturing (CAM), Computer-Aided Process Planning (CAPP), automated material handling (AGVs), and Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) into a unified smart manufacturing loop.

5. Database Information System

  • An organized collection of interrelated data stored centrally or across networks with controlled redundancy to serve multiple business applications simultaneously.
  • Governed by a Database Management System (DBMS) ensuring ACID properties (Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, Durability), data integrity, concurrent access control, and robust disaster backup.
