2.1 Introduction to Personnel Management

  • Personnel Management — branch of management concerned with obtaining, developing, compensating, integrating, and maintaining the enterprise workforce to accomplish organizational objectives effectively.
  • Nature — pervasive across all functional areas, people-centered, continuous, and service-oriented.
  • Objectives:
  • Secure optimal utilization of human resources.
  • Establish and maintain productive, harmonious organizational relationships.
  • Provide maximum personal development opportunities for employees.
  • Reconcile individual employee goals with organizational objectives.

2.2 Functions of Personnel Management

Management functions in personnel are divided into Managerial and Operative functions.

1. Managerial Functions

  • Planning — forecasting human resource requirements, formulating personnel policies, and designing recruitment/development programs.
  • Organizing — grouping personnel activities, establishing role hierarchies, assigning responsibilities, and delegating authority.
  • Directing — leading, motivating, commanding, and supervising subordinates to execute work efficiently.
  • Controlling — auditing personnel performance, measuring results against policies/budgets, and implementing corrective measures.

2. Operative Functions

  • Procurement / Employment — acquiring required workforce: job analysis, manpower planning, recruitment, selection, placement, and induction.
  • Development — elevating employee competence through skills training, executive development, and performance appraisal.
  • Compensation — establishing fair remuneration structures: job evaluation, wage/salary determination, incentive schemes, and bonuses.
  • Welfare Activities — providing safe physical working conditions, canteens, rest rooms, medical care, recreation, and housing.
  • Integration — aligning individual and group interests with organizational goals through collective bargaining, grievance redressing, and disciplinary handling.
  • Maintenance — retaining skilled workforce by preserving physical/mental health, social security benefits, safety measures, and personnel records.

2.3 Development of Personnel Policy

  • Personnel Policy — pre-established, authoritative guide formulated by management to direct decisions regarding human resource actions.
  • Types of Personnel Policies:
  • General Policies — broad philosophical declarations reflecting top management's core philosophy (e.g., equal employment opportunity).
  • Specific Policies — explicit guidelines for specific HR areas (hiring, promotion, compensation, training, termination).
  • Hiring & Employment Status Policies — criteria governing probation periods, permanent status, promotion ladders, and retirement.
  • Everyday Procedural Policies — routine rules governing daily operations: shift timings, attendance, sick leave, overtime, and grievance reporting.

Characteristics of Sound Personnel Policy

  • Clear & Definite — written in plain, unambiguous language avoiding misunderstandings.
  • Stable yet Flexible — consistent over time to build employee trust, yet adaptable to changing environmental conditions.
  • Equitable & Fair — applied uniformly to all employees without favoritism or discrimination.
  • Consistent with Enterprise Objectives — directly aligned with corporate mission and legal labor frameworks.

Steps in Formulating Personnel Policy

  1. Identify the Need — recognizing existing operational difficulties, high turnover, or regulatory shifts requiring explicit policy.
  2. Collect Data & Information — analyzing internal workplace facts, industry benchmarks, and labor laws.
  3. Consult Stakeholders — discussing proposed policies with departmental heads, supervisors, and trade union representatives.
  4. Draft the Policy — formulating clear, precise written policy statements.
  5. Top Management Approval — submitting draft to Board of Directors or CEO for formal review and sanction.
  6. Communicate Policy — disseminating policy to all employees via employee handbooks, notice boards, and orientation sessions.
  7. Evaluate & Review — periodic auditing of policy effectiveness and revising to meet emerging operational needs.

2.4 Manpower Planning (Human Resource Planning)

  • Manpower Planning — strategic process of determining future human capital requirements and ensuring the organization has the right number of qualified people in the right jobs at the right time.
  • Importance:
  • Prevents both labor shortages (production bottlenecks) and labor surplus (excess payroll costs).
  • Facilitates expansion, diversification, and adoption of modern technological innovations.
  • Forms foundation for all subsequent recruitment, training, and succession planning programs.

Manpower Planning Procedure / Steps

  1. Analyze Organizational Objectives & Plans — reviewing corporate growth targets, production schedules, and technology upgrades.
  2. Demand Forecasting — estimating future personnel requirements across skill categories using managerial judgment, ratio-trend analysis, or work-study techniques.
  3. Supply Forecasting — analyzing internal talent inventory (skills inventory, expected promotions, retirements, turnover) and external labor market availability.
  4. Determine Net Manpower Requirements (Gap Analysis):
  5. Surplus Manpower → implement hiring freezes, voluntary retirement schemes (VRS), or redeployment.
  6. Deficit Manpower → initiate recruitment, training, and promotion pipelines.
  7. Formulate Action Plans — executing recruitment, selection, training, transfer, or retention strategies.

2.5 Recruitment and Selection of Manpower

Recruitment

  • Recruitment — positive process of discovering potential applicants and stimulating them to apply for job vacancies.
  • Sources of Recruitment:
  • Internal Sources:
    • Promotions — vertical movement to higher responsibility; boosts morale and loyalty.
    • Transfers — horizontal movement across departments without change in rank/pay; balances departmental staffing.
  • External Sources:
    • Factory Gate Hiring — direct placement of casual/unskilled labor assembling at gate.
    • Advertisements — public notices in press, trade journals, and digital job portals.
    • Educational Institutions (Campus Recruitment) — hiring fresh technical and managerial graduates.
    • Employment Agencies & Consultancies — professional headhunters sourcing specialized talent.
    • Labor Contractors — recruiting temporary manual labor for project-based work.

Scientific Selection

  • Selection — negative process of evaluating applicants and choosing the best-fitted candidates while rejecting unsuitable ones.
  • Steps in Scientific Selection Process:
  • Preliminary Interview & Application Screening — screening out unqualified applicants through brief initial review.
  • Application Blank — comprehensive standardized form capturing educational, work, and personal history.
  • Selection / Employment Tests:
    • Intelligence Tests — measure general mental capacity and reasoning.
    • Aptitude Tests — measure latent potential to learn new skills.
    • Proficiency / Trade Tests — measure existing technical capability and practical performance.
    • Personality Tests — assess emotional balance, sociability, and temperament.
  • Employment / Selection Interview — in-depth face-to-face evaluation of applicant communication, depth, and cultural fit.
  • Reference & Background Checks — verifying candidate character, past employment history, and integrity with prior employers.
  • Physical / Medical Examination — assessing physical fitness required for job and protecting against pre-existing claims.
  • Final Job Offer & Appointment Letter — formal issuance of employment contract specifying terms, wages, and start date.
  • Placement & Induction (Orientation) — introducing employee to job role, workplace rules, company culture, and coworkers.

Comparison: Recruitment vs Selection

Aspect Recruitment Selection
Nature of Process Positive (encourages maximum people to apply) Negative (rejects unsuitable candidates)
Objective Creates large talent pool for selection Selects the best candidate for the vacancy
Sequence Precedes selection Follows recruitment
Contractual Status No employment relationship created Results in legally binding employment contract
Applicant Pool Size Broad, unrestricted pool Progressively filtered to single/few individuals

2.6 Training and Development of Manpower

Core Concepts & Benefits

  • Training — systematic process of teaching specific job skills, knowledge, and operational techniques to employees.
  • Development — broader, long-term educational process designed to enhance conceptual and human competencies for career growth.
  • Benefits of Training:
  • To Organization: Higher productivity, improved quality, reduced supervision, lower accident rates, operational flexibility.
  • To Employees: Increased market value/earning power, enhanced safety, promotion prospects, job satisfaction.

Types of Training

  • Induction / Orientation Training — familiarizing new recruits with organizational layout, rules, policies, and team members.
  • Job Training — teaching specific machine operations, tools, and work methods for assigned tasks.
  • Promotional Training — preparing high-performing employees to assume higher managerial responsibilities.
  • Refresher Training — retraining existing staff to update skills on new technologies and counteract skill obsolescence.
  • Safety Training — instructing workers on operating hazards, protective equipment, and emergency procedures.

Methods of Training

  2. Job Instruction Training (JIT) — structured step-by-step method: prepare worker, present operation, try out performance, follow up.
  3. Coaching & Mentoring — direct daily guidance from experienced superior.
  4. Job Rotation — moving trainee through varied departments to broaden multi-functional competence.
  5. Apprenticeship — long-term blend of classroom instruction and practical craft work under master craftsman.
  6. Off-the-Job Training — training conducted away from active production line:

  9. Case Studies & Role Playing — real-world problem scenarios developing analytical and interpersonal negotiation skills.
  10. Simulations — computerized flight/plant simulators replicating high-risk operating conditions.

2.7 Job Analysis, Job Evaluation, and Merit Rating

1. Job Analysis

  • Job Analysis — systematic procedure of collecting, analyzing, and recording all facts pertaining to a job's duties, responsibilities, working conditions, and required human competencies.
  • Job Analysis Process: (1) Identify jobs → (2) Determine information needed → (3) Collect data → (4) Review with incumbents → (5) Produce Job Description & Specification.
  • Methods of Job Analysis:
  • Observation Method — analyst observes worker directly; best for routine, repetitive physical manual work.
  • Interview Method — analyst questions worker and supervisor regarding duties and problems.
  • Questionnaire Method — structured survey completed by job incumbents; cost-effective for large workforce.
  • Log / Daily Diary Method — employee maintains daily chronological activity log over set period.

Job Description vs Job Specification

Job Analysis yields two distinct documents: - Job Description (Job-Centric) — written statement of what the job entails: - Job Title, Code, Department, and Location. - Job Summary and Objective. - Specific Duties, Tasks, and Responsibilities. - Working Conditions, Environmental Hazards, and Tools/Machinery used. - Job Specification (Person-Centric) — written statement of human qualifications needed: - Educational qualifications and training required. - Prior work experience and technical skill level. - Physical attributes (stamina, vision) and mental abilities (analytical, judgment). - Interpersonal and behavioral traits.

2. Job Evaluation

  • Job Evaluation — systematic process of assessing the relative worth/value of different jobs within an enterprise to establish an equitable, rational internal wage structure.
  • Core Principle — evaluates the job, not the person performing it (Rates the job, not the worker).
  • Methods of Job Evaluation:
  • Non-Quantitative (Qualitative) Methods:
    1. Ranking Method — entire jobs are ranked hierarchically from simplest to hardest based on overall complexity; simple but subjective.
    2. Grading / Classification Method — jobs placed into predetermined grades or classes (e.g., Clerk I, Clerk II, Senior Executive).
  • Quantitative Methods:
    1. Point Rating Method — jobs broken into compensable factors (skill, effort, responsibility, working conditions); numerical points assigned to each factor degree and summed to establish wage brackets.
    2. Factor Comparison Method — key benchmark jobs ranked factor by factor, assigning monetary wage values directly to individual factors.

3. Merit Rating (Performance Appraisal)

  • Merit Rating / Performance Appraisal — systematic evaluation of an individual employee's actual performance, capabilities, and future potential against established job standards (Rates the worker, not the job).
  • Objectives: Determine salary increments, bonuses, and promotions; identify training needs; provide constructive feedback.
  • Traditional Appraisal Methods:
  • Straight Ranking Method — evaluator ranks subordinates from best to worst.
  • Paired Comparison Method — every employee is compared systematically against every other peer in pairs.
  • Graphic Rating Scale — employee traits (quality, attendance, loyalty) evaluated along continuous scale (e.g., 1–5 or Poor to Excellent).
  • Checklist Method — evaluator checks off series of descriptive statements regarding employee behavior.
  • Critical Incident Method — supervisor records specific outstanding positive or negative employee behaviors.
  • Free Essay Method — supervisor writes open-ended narrative appraisal describing employee strengths and weaknesses.
  • Modern Appraisal Methods:
  • Management by Objectives (MBO) — supervisor and employee mutually establish clear, measurable performance goals; appraisal evaluates actual results against objectives.
  • 360-Degree Appraisal — comprehensive feedback gathered anonymously from all surrounding stakeholders: superiors, peers, subordinates, and external clients.
  • Behaviorally Anchored Rating Scales (BARS) — combines rating scale with critical incident behavior descriptions to ensure objective grading.

2.8 Wages and Incentives

Concepts & Layers of Wages

  • Wage — monetary remuneration paid to workers for labor services rendered (typically hourly, daily, or piece-based; monthly/weekly pay for white-collar staff is termed Salary).
  • Three Layers of Wages:
  • Minimum Wage — statutory wage floor required to sustain basic survival, food, shelter, and health for worker living below poverty line.
  • Fair Wage — wage above minimum wage that ensures equal pay for equal work, reflecting enterprise capacity and regional prevailing rates.
  • Living Wage — highest wage standard ensuring maintenance of reasonable comfort, health, social security, and family decency in locality.

Factors Governing Wage and Salary Structure

  • Enterprise financial ability to pay.
  • Demand and supply dynamics of labor in the market.
  • Cost of living indices and inflation trends.
  • Prevailing market wage rates across competing local firms.
  • Trade union bargaining strength.
  • Job requirements, complexity, and risk levels determined via job evaluation.

Wage Payment Systems

  1. Time Rate System:
  2. Wages computed strictly based on duration worked (hours, days, months), independent of output quantity.
  3. Formula: Earnings = Hours Worked × Hourly Wage Rate.
  4. Advantages: Guaranteed income stability, superior product craftsmanship, reduced machinery wear.
  5. Disadvantages: No incentive for high output, treats efficient and lazy workers identically, requires close supervision.
  6. Piece Rate System:
  7. Wages computed strictly based on number of acceptable units completed.
  8. Formula: Earnings = Units Produced × Piece Rate per Unit.
  9. Advantages: Strong direct incentive to boost output, distinguishes efficient workers, simplifies unit labor costing.
  10. Disadvantages: Quality sacrificed for quantity, high equipment wear, potential worker fatigue and earnings volatility.
  11. Combined Time and Piece Rate System:
  12. Guarantees minimum baseline weekly/monthly wage for full working attendance; adds piece-rate bonus for output surpassing standard threshold.

Incentives

  • Incentive — supplementary stimulus or reward designed to motivate employees to exceed standard performance levels.
  • Financial Incentives — direct monetary bonuses, profit sharing, commission, overtime pay, and performance increments.
  • Non-Financial Incentives — flexible working hours, job security, recognition certificates, promotion opportunities, healthcare benefits, subsidized meals, recreational clubs, educational development, and family welfare events.

⚡ Quick Recap

  • Personnel Management blends Managerial (Planning, Organizing, Directing, Controlling) and Operative (Procurement, Development, Compensation, Welfare, Integration, Maintenance) functions.
  • Personnel Policies provide clear, stable, written decision guidelines ensuring consistent, non-discriminatory HR practices.
  • Manpower Planning forecasts future labor demand and internal supply to eliminate talent deficits or payroll surpluses.
  • Recruitment is positive (attracting candidate volume); Selection is negative (systematic elimination via tests, interviews, and medical checks).
  • Training targets immediate job skills (On-the-Job: JIT, apprenticeships; Off-the-Job: vestibule, simulations); Development builds long-term managerial abilities.
  • Job Analysis yields Job Description (tasks and working conditions) and Job Specification (human skills and qualifications).
  • Job Evaluation rates the job to set fair wage structures; Merit Rating rates the employee to determine promotions and training needs.
  • Wages progress from Minimum to Fair to Living Wage; paid via Time Rate (quality/stability) or Piece Rate (quantity/incentive).
