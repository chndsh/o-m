1.1 Organization

Definition & Nature of Organization

  • Organization — social system composed of people, structure, and technology working cooperatively toward common goals in dynamic environment.
  • Key Definitions:
  • Amitai Etzioni: Planned units deliberately structured for attaining specific goals.
  • John R. Schermerhorn: Collection of people working together to achieve common purpose.
  • Keith Davis: Group operating under executive leadership to accomplish common objectives.
  • W.J. Duncan: Interacting, interdependent individuals working toward common goals via defined structure.
  • Core Dimensions — Goal-oriented system (specific objectives), Psychological system (group dynamics), Technological system (knowledge/tools), and Integrated structural activities.

Comparison: Traditional vs Modern Organization

Aspect Traditional Organization Modern Organization
Flexibility Stable, inflexible, rule-oriented Dynamic, flexible, customer-oriented
Work Definition Defined by job position / individual Defined by tasks / team-oriented
Employment Permanent jobs, specific working hours Temporary/fluid jobs, flexible time/place
Hierarchy & Power Command-oriented, top-down decisions Involvement-oriented, participative decisions
Workforce Relatively homogeneous Diverse
Relationships Strict vertical hierarchy Lateral, alterable network relationships

1.1.1 System Approach Applied to Organization

  • System — set of interrelated, interdependent parts forming unified whole.
  • Open System — dynamically interacts with external environment; all business organizations are open systems and must adapt to shifting customer needs, markets, and competition to survive.
  • Closed System — insulated from and does not interact with external environment.
  • System Model Flow: Inputs (Information, Energy, Materials) → Transformation Process (Men-Machine System) → Outputs (Goods, Services, Information) + Feedback Loop.
  • Key System Implications:
  • Parts must coordinate for whole organization to function.
  • Decisions in one subsystem directly impact other subsystems.
  • Organization is dynamic, vulnerable, and must constantly adapt to external environment.

1.1.2 Necessity of Organization

  • Collective Capability — accomplishes tasks impossible for individuals working alone.
  • Scale & Complexity Handling — manages increasing factory size and complex production methods.
  • Competitiveness & Cost Control — optimizes labor and resources to minimize production costs and meet market delivery schedules.
  • Administrative Facilitation — establishes authority delegation, communication, coordination, growth, and diversification.
  • Andrew Carnegie Quote: Physical assets can be rebuilt in years if organization remains; failure/success hinges on organizational strength.

1.1.3 Principles of Organization (Koontz & Weihrich)

  1. Principle of Unity of Goals — all individual and departmental goals must align without contradiction to enterprise objectives.
  2. Principle of Span of Control — number of direct subordinates a manager can effectively supervise is limited.
  3. Scalar Principle (Chain of Command) — unbroken, clearly defined line of authority must run from top executive to lowest employee.
  4. Principle of Unity of Command — each subordinate must receive orders from and report to only one boss.
  5. Principle of Delegation of Authority — authority delegated must match expected task responsibility; managers must resolve issues within their scope.
  6. Principle of Parity of Authority & Responsibility — authority granted must equal responsibility expected (Authority = Responsibility).
  7. Principle of Absoluteness of Responsibility — superior cannot evade ultimate responsibility for subordinate actions; subordinate accountability is absolute.
  8. Principle of Departmentation — activities, authority, and expected results must be clearly demarcated into departmental units.
  9. Principle of Balance — balance must exist across organizational size, departmental span, and degree of centralization.
  10. Principle of Flexibility — structure must adapt smoothly to environmental and technological shifts.
  11. Principle of Separation — employees must not report to the department whose work they evaluate/audit.
  12. Principle of Leadership Facilitation — structure must nurture leadership growth and decision autonomy.

1.1.4 Formal and Informal Organizations

  • Formal Organization — deliberately created structure of roles, authority, and scalar communication designed by management to accomplish specific goals (Koontz & Weihrich).
  • Informal Organization — spontaneous network of personal and social relationships emerging naturally from human interactions and proximity (Keith Davis).

Comparison: Formal vs Informal Organization

Feature Formal Organization Informal Organization
Origin Deliberately planned by management Spontaneous, unplanned social emergence
Structure Defined by organization chart / hierarchy Unstructured, network of interpersonal ties
Authority Delegated top-down through scalar chain Conferred by peer acceptance / social norms
Communication Formal channels / scalar chain Grapevine / informal chatter
Rules & Conduct Written policies, procedures, and manuals Unwritten group norms, customs, values
Adaptability Slow, rigid adaptation to external changes Rapid adaptation to change, but resists internal change
Focus Job, efficiency, and goal attainment Individual needs, satisfaction, and friendship

1.2 Management

Definition & Scope

  • Management — process of planning, organizing, staffing, leading, and controlling available resources (5Ms: Men, Money, Material, Method, Machine) to achieve predetermined objectives efficiently and effectively.
  • Business Perspective — securing maximum results with minimum efforts for mutual prosperity of employer, employee, and public.
  • F.W. Taylor Definition: Knowing exactly what you want people to do, then seeing that they do it in the best and cheapest way.

1.2.1 Functions of Management

  1. Planning — deciding in advance what, how, when, and who will perform tasks; selecting optimal future courses of action among alternatives.
  2. Organizing — defining tasks, grouping into departments, assigning personnel, establishing authority relationships, and coordinating efforts.
  3. Staffing — acquiring and deploying human capital; involves manpower planning, recruitment, selection, job analysis, training, appraisal, and compensation.
  4. Leading (Directing) — guiding, stimulating, communicating with, and motivating workforce to achieve organizational objectives.
  5. Controlling — 4-stage cyclical process ensuring actual performance matches standards:
  6. (a) Establishing standards → (b) Measuring actual performance → (c) Finding & analyzing deviations → (d) Taking corrective actions.

1.2.2 Levels of Management

        / \
       /   \      Top Level (BOD, CEO, MD, GM) — Conceptual & Strategic
      /-----\
     /       \    Middle Level (HODs, Branch Managers) — Tactical & Interpersonal
    /---------\
   /           \  Lower / First-Line (Supervisors, Foremen) — Technical & Operational
  /-------------\
  • Top Level Management (BOD, CEO, President):
  • Formulates long-term plans (5–20 years), vision, and master policies; allocates broad resources.
  • Final authority accountable to shareholders, government, and society.
  • Middle Level Management (HODs, Junior Executives):
  • Executes top-level policies; formulates medium-term plans (1–5 years).
  • Acts as intermediary buffer; coordinates interdepartmental workflows.
  • Lower / Supervisory Level Management (Foremen, Supervisors):
  • Directly supervises operative workers; prepares daily, weekly, and monthly operating schedules.
  • Handles shop-floor problem solving, worker morale, and upward communication of grievances.

1.2.3 Managerial Skills (Robert Katz)

  • Technical Skills — ability to use tools, techniques, and procedures in specialized field; highest importance at lower/supervisory management.
  • Human Skills — interpersonal competence to motivate, communicate, lead, and resolve conflict; critical across all management levels.
  • Conceptual Skills — mental ability to visualize organization as whole, understand subsystem interdependence, and think strategically; highest importance at top management.

Comparison: Skill Distribution Across Management Levels

Management Level Primary Focus Technical Skills Human Skills Conceptual Skills
Top Level Strategic vision & policy Low High Very High
Middle Level Departmental coordination Moderate Very High Moderate
Lower Level Operational execution Very High High Low

1.2.4 Importance of Management

  • Achieving Group Goals — channels unorganized resources into focused collective enterprise.
  • Optimum Resource Utilization — selects best alternative usage for scarce human, physical, and financial capital.
  • Cost Reduction — minimizes input expenses while maximizing productive output via efficiency.
  • Sound Organizational Structure — eliminates role ambiguity by establishing clear authority-responsibility links.
  • Environmental Adaptation — maintains equilibrium amidst external market, legal, and technological turbulence.
  • Social Prosperity — generates employment, elevates living standards, and produces quality products at reasonable costs.

1.2.5 Models of Management

  1. Hierarchical Model — relies on formal authority and downward delegation; simple algorithmic structure, but ignores charisma and horizontal communication.
  2. Task-Oriented Model — sequences managerial activities chronologically: plan → organize → direct → control.
  3. Allocation Model — focuses on acquiring, allocating, monitoring, and balancing resource stocks (5Ms).
  4. Transactional Model — views management as value-adding stage in transformation chain converting raw inputs to products.
  5. Team Effort Model — treats manager as coach/leader orchestrating interlocking groups through cooperation and mutual trust.
  6. Knowledge-Oriented Model — focuses on accumulating organizational intellectual property and diffusing expertise via mentoring/training.
  7. Goal-Concentrated Model — aligns internal/external environmental signals with goal adjustments via continuous feedback loops.

1.3 Theory of Management

Evolution & Thinkers Timeline

  • Charles Babbage (1792–1871) — advocated division of labor, scientific work methods, profit sharing, and time-and-motion studies.
  • F.W. Taylor (1856–1915) — "Father of Scientific Management"; shop-floor efficiency and scientific task standardization.
  • Henry Gantt (1861–1919) — developed Gantt chart for production scheduling and task-and-bonus wage system.
  • Henri Fayol (1841–1925) — "Father of Modern Management / Administrative Theory"; 14 principles of general management.
  • Max Weber (1864–1920) — developed Theory of Bureaucracy based on rational-legal authority.

1.3.1 Scientific Management Approach (F.W. Taylor)

  • Core Concept — replaces rule-of-thumb guesswork with scientific investigation to identify the "one best way" to perform a job.
  • Taylor's Four Principles:
  • Develop science for each element of work (replacing rule-of-thumb).
  • Scientifically select, train, and develop workers.
  • Cooperate heartily with workers to ensure work matches scientific principles.
  • Divide work and responsibility equally between management (planning) and workers (doing).
  • Key Elements & Tools:
  • Separation of Planning and Doing — planning by specialists; execution by workers.
  • Functional Foremanship — 8 specialized bosses (4 Planning: Instruction Card Clerk, Route Clerk, Time & Cost Clerk, Disciplinarian; 4 Execution: Gang Boss, Speed Boss, Repair Boss, Inspector).
  • Work Study — Time study (stopwatch timing), Motion study (eliminating wasted movements), Fatigue study (determining rest intervals), Method study (finding best way).
  • Standardization — uniform tools, raw materials, and working conditions.
  • Differential Piece-Rate Wage System — high piece rate for workers hitting/exceeding standard; low piece rate for sub-standard performance.
  • Contributions — standardized mass production, efficient job design, objective performance measurements.
  • Limitations — mechanistic view treating workers like cogs; ignores social/psychological needs; violates unity of command via functional foremanship.

1.3.2 Administrative Management Approach (Henri Fayol)

  • Focus — macro-level organizational administration from top management perspective.
  • Six Primary Business Activities:
  • Technical (Production/manufacturing)
  • Commercial (Buying, selling, exchange)
  • Financial (Capital acquisition & control)
  • Security (Protection of property & personnel)
  • Accounting (Recording, balance sheets, costing, statistics)
  • Managerial (Planning, organizing, commanding, coordinating, controlling)

Fayol's 14 Principles of Management

  1. Division of Work — specialization yields greater quantity and quality of output with same effort.
  2. Authority and Responsibility — right to command coupled with accountability; official authority must match personal integrity.
  3. Discipline — obedience, respect for agreements, and judicious application of penalties.
  4. Unity of Command — each employee receives instructions from one superior only.
  5. Unity of Direction — one head and one plan for all operations sharing common objective.
  6. Subordination of Individual Interest to General Interest — enterprise objectives override personal interest.
  7. Remuneration — fair, equitable compensation providing maximum satisfaction to employee and employer.
  8. Centralization — optimum balance between top-level control and subordinate initiative depending on circumstances.
  9. Scalar Chain — unbroken hierarchy from top to bottom; allows Gang Plank (cross-communication bridge) to bypass delays if authorized.
  10. Order — systematic placement: material order ("place for everything, everything in its place") and social order ("right person in right place").
  11. Equity — kindness, impartiality, and justice from managers toward subordinates.
  12. Stability of Tenure of Personnel — minimal employee turnover ensures stability and organizational learning.
  13. Initiative — encouraging employees to conceive and execute plans bolsters zeal and commitment.
  14. Esprit de Corps — promoting team spirit and mutual harmony ("union is strength"); avoid divide-and-rule.

Bureaucracy Theory (Max Weber)

  • Concept — rational-legal model of organizational structure characterized by hierarchy, written rules, and impersonal authority.
  • Core Characteristics:
  • Clearly defined hierarchy of authority.
  • Division of labor based on functional specialization.
  • Comprehensive system of abstract rules and procedures applied uniformly.
  • Impersonality in interpersonal relationships (objective, emotion-free decisions).
  • Career advancement based on technical qualifications, exams, and merit.
  • Advantages — consistency, predictability, fairness, clear role definitions.
  • Disadvantages — red tape, procedural rigidity, resistance to change, goal displacement (rules become ends rather than means).

1.3.3 Behavioral Management Approach

  • Premise — organizations are psycho-social systems; human element is paramount over pure physical/mechanistic efficiency.
  • Pioneers: Robert Owen (humanitarian treatment of labor), Hugo Münsterberg (industrial psychology applied to worker selection).

Hawthorne Studies (Elton Mayo & Associates, 1924–1932)

  1. Illumination Experiments (1924–1927) — tested impact of light intensity; productivity rose across both test and control groups even when lighting dropped to moonlight level.
  2. Relay Assembly Test Room (1927–1929) — manipulated rest breaks, work hours, and incentives; output rose steadily regardless of physical variations because workers felt valued and singled out.
  3. Mass Interviewing Program (1928–1930) — discovered worker morale is governed by informal social groups and psychological attitudes rather than company rules.
  4. Bank Wiring Observation Room (1932) — 14 men restricted output to social norm ("fair day's work") to protect slow peers and counter management rate cuts; social sanctions governed output.
  5. Hawthorne Effect — tendency of individuals to alter behavior and improve performance when they receive special attention and know they are being observed.
  6. Major Conclusions:
  7. Productivity is driven by psychological and social satisfaction, not physical conditions alone.
  8. Informal group norms exert stronger influence on worker output than formal managerial directives.
  9. Non-financial incentives (recognition, dignity, belonging) often outweigh direct financial incentives.
  10. Criticisms: Controlled laboratory settings lacked external validity; overemphasized social harmony while downplaying technology, conflict, and economic drivers.

Mary Parker Follett: Participatory Management

  • Management is getting things done through people by motivating rather than demanding.
  • Four Principles of Coordination:
  • Coordination by direct contact with people involved.
  • Coordination in early stages of planning and policy.
  • Coordination as reciprocal relating of all factors in situation.
  • Coordination as continuous ongoing process.

Behavioral Science Theories

  • Abraham Maslow (Needs Theory) — five hierarchical levels of human needs: Physiological → Safety → Social → Esteem → Self-Actualization.
  • Douglas McGregor (Theory X and Theory Y):
  • Theory X (Pessimistic / Authoritarian) — assumes employees inherently dislike work, avoid responsibility, lack ambition, and require coercion and close supervision.
  • Theory Y (Optimistic / Participative) — assumes work is natural like play, employees seek responsibility, possess creativity, and achieve self-direction when committed to goals.

1.3.4 Modern Management Theories

  1. Quantitative Approach (Management Science / Operations Research):
  2. Formulates mathematical models, equations, and simulations for decision-making.
  3. Evaluates decisions using measurable economic criteria (cost reduction, return on investment, inventory optimization).
  4. System Approach:
  5. Organization viewed as open, purposeful, dynamic system composed of interrelated subsystems (departments).
  6. Holistic synergy: total system output is greater than the sum of isolated parts (Synergy: 2 + 2 > 4).
  7. Contingency (Situational) Approach (Fiedler, Woodward, Lorsch, Lawrence):
  8. Rejects "one best way" / universal principles; managerial action is contingent upon specific environmental conditions.
  9. Four-step diagnostic cycle: (1) Analyze situation → (2) Evaluate theoretical applicability → (3) Match technique to situation → (4) Implement.
  10. Core contingency variables: External environment, Technology, Organization size, People.
  11. Operational Approach (Koontz & Weihrich) — synthesizes concepts from empirical, behavioral, systems, and mathematical schools around core managerial functions.

1.4 Forms of Ownership

Overview of Business Ownership

  • Business ownership type determines legal status, capital base, liability distribution, taxation, and operational freedom.

1.4.1 Single Ownership (Sole Proprietorship)

  • Definition — enterprise owned, financed, and controlled by a single individual who bears all risks and pockets all net profits.
  • Advantages:
  • Simple, inexpensive formation and dissolution (minimal legal formalities).
  • Absolute managerial control and prompt decision-making.
  • Complete business secrecy (no public financial disclosures).
  • Direct profit motivation (100% financial upside).
  • Limitations:
  • Unlimited Liability — owner's personal assets can be seized to pay business debts.
  • Limited financial resources (capped by owner's personal wealth/credit).
  • Lack of business continuity (illness, death, or insolvency dissolves firm).
  • Limited managerial abilities across diverse functions.

1.4.2 Partnership

  • Definition — voluntary association of 2 to 20 persons agreeing to pool capital, labor, and skill to conduct business for mutual profit under a Partnership Deed.
  • Types of Partners:
  • General Partner — participates actively; unlimited joint and several liability.
  • Limited Partner — liability strictly capped at invested capital; cannot participate in daily management.
  • Active / Working Partner — contributes capital and manages daily operations.
  • Sleeping / Dormant Partner — contributes capital and shares profits/losses, but takes no active management role.
  • Nominal Partner — lends name/reputation to firm without contributing capital or sharing profits.
  • Advantages:
  • Greater capital pooling and borrowing capacity than sole proprietorship.
  • Combined managerial skills, specialized division of responsibilities, and balanced judgment.
  • Flexibility of operations and ease of formation.
  • Limitations:
  • Unlimited Joint and Several Liability for general partners (one partner's contract binds all).
  • Risk of interpersonal conflict, mistrust, and deadlock.
  • Lack of continuity (death, retirement, or bankruptcy of one partner terminates partnership).
  • Difficulty in transferring ownership share without unanimous consent of all partners.

1.4.3 Joint Stock Company

  • Definition — artificial legal entity created by law, possessing independent legal personality, common seal, perpetual succession, and share capital with limited liability.
  • Key Characteristics:
  • Separate Legal Entity — company can own property, sue, and be sued in its corporate name.
  • Perpetual Succession — life of company unaffected by death, insolvency, or exit of shareholders.
  • Limited Liability — shareholder liability capped strictly at unpaid value of subscribed shares.
  • Democratic Management — ownership separated from management; managed by elected Board of Directors (BOD).
  • Common Seal — serves as official signature of company on legal contracts.

Comparison: Private vs Public Limited Company

Feature Private Limited Company (Pvt. Ltd.) Public Limited Company (Ltd.)
Number of Members Min: 1, Max: 101 Min: 7, Max: Unlimited
Minimum Directors 2 3
Minimum Paid-up Capital No statutory minimum prescribed Statutory minimum required (e.g., Rs. 1 Crore)
Share Transferability Restricted among members; no public sale Freely transferable on open stock exchanges
Public Subscription Strictly prohibited from inviting public funds Openly invites public to buy shares/debentures
Statutory Meeting Not mandatory Mandatory within prescribed timeframe

Formation & Registration Steps of Joint Stock Company

  1. Promotion Stage — promoters conceive business idea, conduct feasibility studies, and prepare preliminary contracts.
  2. Document Preparation:
  3. Memorandum of Association (MOA) — charter defining company's external scope, name, registered office, objectives, capital, and liability.
  4. Articles of Association (AOA) — bylaws governing internal management, voting rules, and director powers.
  5. Prospectus — formal document inviting public subscription to shares.
  6. Incorporation Stage — filing documents with Registrar of Companies; obtaining Certificate of Incorporation (legal birth).
  7. Capital Subscription Stage — raising minimum subscription from public (mandatory for public companies).
  8. Commencement of Business — obtaining Certificate of Commencement of Business to start operations.

Advantages & Disadvantages of Joint Stock Company

  • Advantages: Colossal capital accumulation, limited liability, perpetual life, professional management, free share liquidity.
  • Disadvantages: Complex registration, heavy regulatory compliance, double taxation, lack of secrecy, operational delays, potential management-owner conflict of interest.

1.4.4 Co-operative Societies

  • Definition — voluntary, autonomous association of individuals united voluntarily to meet common economic, social, and cultural needs through mutually-owned, democratically-controlled enterprise.
  • Guiding Principle — "Each for all and all for each"; service motive overrides pure profit motive.
  • Core Features: Voluntary open membership, democratic management ("One member, one vote" regardless of share count), limited return on capital, equitable dividend distribution based on patronage.
  • Types of Co-operatives:
  • Consumer Co-operatives — eliminate retail middlemen to supply goods to members at fair prices.
  • Producer Co-operatives — assist small producers by pooling raw materials, machinery, and production tools.
  • Marketing Co-operatives — aggregate smallholder produce to secure better bargaining power and market prices.
  • Credit Co-operatives — provide low-interest credit to protect members from exploitative moneylenders.
  • Housing Co-operatives — construct or procure residential plots/homes for members at subsidized rates.
  • Farming Co-operatives — pool fragmented land holdings for mechanized, large-scale agriculture.
  • Advantages: Democratic equality, open membership, lower operational costs, social upliftment, government concessions.
  • Limitations: Inadequate capital base, amateur managerial skills, internal political rivalry, excessive state regulation.

1.4.5 Public Corporations (Statutory Corporations)

  • Definition — autonomous corporate entity established under a Special Act of Parliament/Legislature, fully or majority-owned by state, with defined public mandate.
  • Features: Wholly state-financed, separate legal entity, operational autonomy, managed by government-appointed board, non-civil-service personnel.
  • Advantages: Operational autonomy without civil service red tape, public accountability to Parliament, flexibility in commercial operations, protection of national strategic interests.
  • Limitations: Frequent political and ministerial interference, bureaucratic inertia, lack of profit incentive leading to commercial inefficiencies, rigid charter amendments requiring parliamentary approval.

1.5 Organizational Structure

Definition & Core Elements

  • Organizational Structure — formal system of task relationships, workflow pathways, and reporting hierarchies coordinating cooperative human effort.
  • Four Elements of Structure:
  • Specialization & Division of Labor — allocation of specific activities to specialized roles.
  • Departmentation — grouping related tasks and positions into logical units.
  • Span of Control — number of direct subordinates supervised by a single manager (narrow span creates tall hierarchy; wide span creates flat hierarchy).
  • Chain of Command & Delegation — line of authority connecting organizational echelons.

1.5.1 Line Organization (Military / Scalar Organization)

  • Concept — simplest, oldest structure; direct vertical line of authority descends unbroken from top manager to lowest operative.
  • Departmental Line Organization — business divided into distinct functional departments, each headed by a line executive exercising absolute downward authority.
  • Advantages: Simplicity, absolute clarity of authority/responsibility, swift decision-making, strict discipline, lower overhead costs.
  • Disadvantages: Managerial overload, lack of expert specialization, authoritarian tendencies, organizational brittleness upon key executive loss.

1.5.2 Line and Staff Organization

  • Concept — combines executive line authority with advisory staff specialists.
  • Line Managers — hold direct authority and responsibility to issue commands and execute primary objectives.
  • Staff Specialists — provide expert advice, technical research, and counsel to line managers; possess advisory authority only (No command power).
  • Advantages: Combines operational discipline with expert guidance, relieves line executives of specialist burdens, improves decision quality.
  • Disadvantages: Friction and conflict between line and staff, high overhead costs of advisory departments, staff frustration over ignored advice.
  • Causes of Line-Staff Conflict:
  • Staff perceives line managers as conservative, stubborn, and resistant to modern technical advice.
  • Line perceives staff as ivory-tower theorists lacking practical shop-floor realism.
  • Ambiguous role boundaries leading to staff overstepping advisory roles to give direct orders.

1.5.3 Functional Organization (F.W. Taylor)

  • Concept — tasks organized by specialized functions; every specialist supervisor exercises direct functional authority over workers in their specific domain.
  • Characteristics: Complete functional specialization; breaks unity of command (one worker reports to multiple functional bosses).
  • Advantages: Maximum benefits of specialization, high operational efficiency, reduced burden on single executives, ease of worker training.
  • Disadvantages: Complete violation of unity of command, worker confusion from conflicting orders, coordination nightmares, buck-passing of responsibility.

1.5.4 Committee Organization

  • Concept — group of designated individuals formally constituted to deliberate, investigate, advise, or decide on specific matters collectively.
  • Types of Committees:
  • Standing (Permanent) Committee — enduring body handling recurring issues (e.g., Finance Committee, Executive Committee).
  • Ad-hoc (Temporary / Special) Committee — formed for single specific assignment and dissolved upon completion.
  • Advisory Committee — evaluates data and recommends actions without executive enforcement authority.
  • Executive Committee — exercises decision-making and managerial enforcement powers.
  • Advantages: Pooled knowledge and diverse viewpoints, enhanced cooperation and buy-in, checks against autocratic power, effective cross-functional coordination.
  • Disadvantages: Slow decision-making ("committees keep minutes and waste hours"), dilution/buck-passing of responsibility, costly administrative overhead, compromised decisions representing lowest common denominator.

1.6 Purchasing and Marketing Management

1.6.1 Purchasing — Introduction & Principles

  • Purchasing — procuring raw materials, supplies, tools, and equipment needed for manufacturing and enterprise operations.
  • Importance — directly governs production continuity, final product quality, and manufacturing cost (materials often represent 50–70% of total product cost).
  • 5 Rights / Principles of Purchasing:
  • Right Quality — suitability of material specifications for intended purpose.
  • Right Quantity — ordering optimal lot size (EOQ) avoiding shortages and excess carrying costs.
  • Right Price — lowest total cost consistent with acceptable quality and reliable delivery.
  • Right Time — aligning material deliveries with production schedules to avoid downtime.
  • Right Source — selecting dependable vendors with financial stability and quality standards.

1.6.2 Functions of Purchasing Department

  • Procuring Materials — receiving purchase requisitions, sourcing, placing orders, and tracking deliveries.
  • Evaluating Price & Cost Control — analyzing market price trends, vendor quotations, and discounts.
  • Supplier Management & Bidding — evaluating supplier reliability, floating competitive tenders/RFQs, and negotiating contract terms.
  • Policy & Legal Compliance — ensuring purchases adhere to corporate governance, tax regulations, and contract law.
  • Paperwork & Accounting Reconciliation — verifying purchase orders against delivery challans and invoices before payment approval.

1.6.3 Methods of Purchasing

  1. Purchasing by Requirement (Hand-to-Mouth) — buying materials strictly as immediate needs arise; minimizes inventory holding costs, but risks stockouts and misses bulk discounts.
  2. Market Purchasing — buying in advance based on favorable market price trends to capitalize on low rates.
  3. Speculative Purchasing — buying quantities far exceeding operating needs purely to resell at profit when prices climb; carries high financial risk.
  4. Purchasing for Specific Future Period — buying standard materials required for fixed future operating cycle (e.g., 3 or 6 months).
  5. Contract Purchasing — formal long-term agreement locking in price, quality, and delivery terms over extended duration.
  6. Scheduled Purchasing — supplier delivers fixed quantities at predetermined dates matching buyer's production schedule.
  7. Group Purchasing of Small Items — grouping miscellaneous minor items with single vendor to cut purchase order processing overhead.
  8. Co-operative Purchasing — small industrial or agricultural units pooling requirements to leverage bulk volume discounts.

Purchasing Procedure / Steps

  1. Receipt of Purchase Requisition from operating departments.
  2. Exploring supply sources and issuing Requests for Quotation (RFQs) / Tenders.
  3. Receiving quotations and comparative analysis of bids.
  4. Vendor selection and issuing official Purchase Order (PO).
  5. Follow-up and expediting delivery schedules.
  6. Receiving, inspection, and verification of materials against PO specifications.
  7. Invoice checking, verification, and passing for accounting payment.

1.6.4 Marketing — Introduction & Importance

  • Marketing — social and managerial process by which individuals and organizations obtain what they need and want through creating, offering, and exchanging value with others (Philip Kotler).
  • Scope — covers product concept, pricing, distribution, customer service, and feedback; spans from pre-production to post-sales satisfaction.
  • Importance: Connects producers to consumers, boosts revenue, creates employment, stabilizes price fluctuations, drives product innovations, and elevates consumer living standards.

1.6.5 Functions of Marketing

  • Functions of Exchange:
  • Buying & Assembling — procuring varied goods to offer complete assortment to target markets.
  • Selling — finding buyers, stimulating demand through advertising, and negotiating transfer of title.
  • Functions of Physical Supply:
  • Transportation — moving goods from production centers to geographically dispersed consumption points.
  • Storage & Warehousing — preserving goods and balancing seasonal production with continuous demand.
  • Facilitating Functions:
  • Financing — providing credit arrangements across distribution intermediaries.
  • Risk Bearing — absorbing risks of price fluctuations, perishability, obsolescence, and damage.
  • Standardization & Grading — establishing uniform quality standards and classifying goods into uniform grades.
  • Market Research & Information — collecting and analyzing market intelligence regarding consumer preferences and competitor moves.

1.6.6 Advertising

  • Advertising — any paid form of non-personal presentation and promotion of ideas, goods, or services by an identified sponsor (Philip Kotler).
  • Core Objectives:
  • Create brand awareness and introduce new products to market.
  • Differentiate product from competitors and stimulate consumer demand.
  • Build corporate goodwill, brand loyalty, and long-term brand equity.
  • Support sales force and retail dealer networks.
  • Advertising Media:
  • Print Media (Newspapers, magazines, trade journals).
  • Broadcast Media (Television, radio).
  • Outdoor Media (Hoardings, billboards, transit ads).
  • Digital / Online Media (Search engines, social media, websites, email).
  • Direct Mail (Brochures, promotional catalogs sent directly to prospects).
  • Steps in Creating Advertisements:
  • Define advertising objectives (inform, persuade, remind).
  • Identify and analyze target audience.
  • Formulate advertising budget.
  • Develop creative message strategy and copy design.
  • Select appropriate media mix.
  • Execute campaign and measure post-campaign advertising effectiveness.

⚡ Quick Recap

  • Organization is an open socio-technical system requiring goal unity, scalar chain, and balanced formal/informal structures.
  • Management consists of Planning, Organizing, Staffing, Leading, and Controlling applied across 5Ms to achieve organizational objectives.
  • Management Levels demand distinct skill mixes: Top = Conceptual; Middle = Human/Managerial; Lower = Technical.
  • Scientific Management (Taylor) champions time-motion study, functional foremanship, and differential piece rates; Administrative Theory (Fayol) establishes 14 universal principles and 6 core business activities.
  • Hawthorne Studies (Mayo) proved social/psychological factors and informal group norms drive worker productivity over physical/financial factors alone.
  • Modern Theories combine Operations Research (Quantitative), Subsystem Synergy (Systems), and Situational Diagnostics (Contingency: no "one best way").
  • Ownership Forms balance liability vs capital: Sole Proprietorship (unlimited liability, maximum secrecy), Partnership (pooled skills, joint unlimited liability), Joint Stock Company (limited liability, massive capital, perpetual life), Co-operatives (mutual service, democratic control).
  • Organizational Structures progress from pure Line (simple, rigid) to Line-and-Staff (advisory experts) and Functional (specialized bosses).
  • Purchasing targets the 5 Rights (Quality, Quantity, Price, Time, Source); Marketing coordinates exchange, physical distribution, and promotional functions.
