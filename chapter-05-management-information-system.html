5.1 Data and Information

Definitions & Transformation

  • Data — raw, unprocessed, unorganized facts, figures, symbols, and observations (Raw material input).
  • Information — data that has been processed, organized, structured, or contextualized to be meaningful, purposeful, and valuable for human decision-making (Finished goods output).
  • Davis and Olson Definition: Data processed into a form meaningful to the recipient, with real or perceived value in current or prospective actions/decisions.
  • Data-to-Information Flow: $$\text{Data (Input)} \longrightarrow \text{Processing (Algorithm / Context)} \longrightarrow \text{Information (Output)} \longleftrightarrow \text{Feedback / Control}$$

Comparison: Data vs Information

Dimension Data Information
Nature Raw, unorganized, uninterpreted facts Processed, contextualized, structured data
Form Figures, text, raw sensor/transaction logs Reports, summaries, charts, actionable insights
Value Low direct value; non-actionable alone High strategic/operational value; guides decisions
Dependency Independent entity; starting point Wholly dependent upon collected data

10 Qualities of Good Information

  1. Timeliness — delivered to decision-makers within required timeframes; delayed information loses decision utility.
  2. Accuracy — error-free, clear, and uncorrupted by biases or computational flaws.
  3. Relevance — directly answers operational/strategic queries (What, Why, Where, When, Who); tailored to managerial need.
  4. Adequacy — sufficient volume to guide decisions without causing information overload (Inadequacy = Crisis; Overload = Chaos).
  5. Completeness — provides all critical facts; missing variables lead to flawed corporate decisions.
  6. Explicitness — clarity requiring no further cognitive decomposition or secondary analysis by recipient.
  7. Impartiality — neutral, unbiased reporting free from predetermined political or executive agendas.
  8. Validity — degree to which data genuinely measures what it purports to measure for specific decision context.
  9. Reliability — consistency and trustworthiness derived from verified sources and sound sampling methodologies.
  10. Consistency — derived from stable, uniform baseline periods and measurement units over time.

5.2 Need, Function, and Importance of MIS

Definition of MIS

  • Management Information System (MIS) — integrated, computer-based system that collects, stores, processes, and disseminates data to provide managers with timely, accurate, and actionable information for planning, controlling, and operational decision-making.

Need of MIS

  • Support Business Processes — automates customer requests, transaction flows, and delivery cycles.
  • Support Daily Operations — provides real-time operational status, maintenance schedules, and resource tracking.
  • Support Managerial Decision-Making — delivers synthesized reports that reduce uncertainty across managerial tiers.
  • Drive Competitive Strategy — tracks market dynamics, competitor positioning, and customer behavior.

5 Primary Functions of MIS

  1. Data Processing — systematic collection, validation, transmission, transformation, and storage of transaction data.
  2. Prediction — projecting future trends and outcomes using mathematical models, statistical forecasting, and regression.
  3. Planning — assisting departmental managers in preparing coordinated sales, inventory, and budget schedules.
  4. Control — comparing actual operational performance against targets, flagging deviations, and generating exception reports.
  5. Decision Assistance — supplying structured alternative evaluations and scenario analyses to managers.

Importance of MIS

  • Supplies tailored information at reasonable economic cost.
  • Eliminates redundant record-keeping and curtails operational paperwork.
  • Secures and protects confidential corporate data assets.
  • Focuses managerial attention on high benefit-to-cost business opportunities.

5.3 Evolution of MIS

Historical Milestones

  [1939–1950s: Dawn of Computing] — Atanasoff-Berry computer; IBM punch card systems (scientific/census data)
         ↓
  [Late 1950s–1960s: EDP Era] — Electronic Data Processing; mainframe transaction automation, card punching
         ↓
  [1970s: Birth of Formal MIS] — Bridging language gap between programmers and business managers; standardized reports
         ↓
  [1980s: DSS & Personal Computing] — PC explosion, interactive Decision Support Systems, Operations Research models
         ↓
  [1990s: EIS & Enterprise Systems] — Executive Information Systems, GUI interfaces, early ERP integrations
         ↓
  [2000s–Present: Advanced IS] — Data mining, Business Intelligence (BI), Cloud, Big Data analytics, AI-driven Expert Systems

Factors Driving MIS Evolution

  • Rapid expansion of business scale and organizational structural complexity.
  • Emergence of sophisticated management theories and quantitative operations research.
  • Exponential increases in computing hardware speed, miniaturization, and memory capacity.
  • Development of relational databases (RDBMS), graphical user interfaces (GUI), and telecommunications networks.

5.4 Organizational Structure and MIS

Pyramidal Information Hierarchy

MIS operates as a multi-tier pyramidal architecture mapped directly against organizational levels:

           / \
          /   \        Strategic Management (Top: CEO, BOD)
         /-----\       - Long-term, unstructured, summary, external focus
        /       \      Tactical Management (Middle: HODs, Branch Managers)
       /---------\     - Medium-term, semi-structured, departmental control
      /           \    Operational Management (Lower: Foremen, Supervisors)
     /-------------\   - Short-term, structured, highly detailed, internal focus
  1. Operational Level (Lower Management):
  2. Focus: Detailed day-to-day transaction tracking, production quotas, material availability, and worker scheduling.
  3. Time Horizon: Hourly, daily, weekly.
  4. Information Characteristics: Highly structured, internal, historical, completely detailed.
  5. Tactical Level (Middle Management):
  6. Focus: Departmental resource allocation, budget tracking, performance variance analysis, and schedule compliance.
  7. Time Horizon: Weekly, monthly, quarterly.
  8. Information Characteristics: Semi-structured, summarized, comparative, operational exception reports.
  9. Strategic Level (Top Management):
  10. Focus: Corporate policy formulation, long-range planning, mergers/acquisitions, and market positioning.
  11. Time Horizon: Quarterly, yearly, 5–20 years.
  12. Information Characteristics: Unstructured, highly aggregated summaries, forward-looking projections, strong external market intelligence.

5.5 Computers and MIS

  • Role of Computers — hardware and software infrastructure enabling rapid execution, massive storage, and error-free retrieval of information.
  • Two Broad Computing Domains:
  • Routine Transaction Processing — batch and real-time processing of high-volume clerical transactions (payroll, billing).
  • End-User Computing — interactive software (spreadsheets, analytics dashboards, query tools) used directly by managers for analysis.
  • Database Management Systems (DBMS):
  • Centralized, organized collection of shared data accessible by multiple corporate applications simultaneously.
  • Personal Database — maintained by an individual user for local productivity.
  • Corporate Database — enterprise-wide repository ensuring unified data integrity, security, and concurrency.
  • Cautionary Rule: Computers do not fix broken organizational processes; introducing computers to disorganized management merely generates computerized confusion (Garbage In, Garbage Out / GIGO).

5.6 Classification of Information Systems

1. Transaction Processing System (TPS)

  • Role — operational-level system capturing and recording routine day-to-day business transactions (orders, payments, payroll, shipping).
  • Features — highly structured, high processing volume, detailed output, baseline input provider for all higher systems.

2. Management Information System (MIS)

  • Role — tactical-level system that aggregates, summarizes, and compares TPS data to generate periodic reports for middle managers.
  • Output Types:
  • Scheduled Reports — regular daily/weekly sales and production summaries.
  • Exception Reports — generated only when metrics breach pre-set thresholds (e.g., expenses exceed 15% of budget).
  • Demand / Ad-hoc Reports — custom queries produced on request.

3. Decision Support System (DSS)

  • Role — interactive, model-driven system assisting senior/middle managers in solving semi-structured and unstructured problems.
  • Key Components: Database (internal/external data), Model Base (mathematical/statistical simulation algorithms), and Dialogue / User Interface.
  • Core Capabilities: "What-If" analysis, Sensitivity analysis, Goal-seeking analysis, and Risk modeling.

4. Executive Support System (ESS) / Executive Information System (EIS)

  • Role — specialized, high-level system tailored specifically for top-echelon executives (CEOs, Board members).
  • Features — intuitive Graphical User Interfaces (dashboards), drill-down capabilities from high-level summaries to raw records, integration of internal metrics with external competitive/economic news.

5. Office Automation System (OAS)

  • Role — applies computer and communication technologies to streamline routine office administration.
  • Applications — word processing, desktop publishing, email, electronic scheduling, document imaging, and video conferencing.
  • User Groups — Clerical personnel (data entry, typing) and Executive professionals (drafting, communicating).

6. Business Expert Systems (ES)

  • Role — advanced artificial intelligence (AI) system that captures human expert heuristics to solve complex problems in specialized domains (credit approvals, fault diagnosis).
  • Three Core Components:
  • Knowledge Base — curated facts, rules, and heuristics.
  • Inference Engine — reasoning brain applying logical deduction to user queries.
  • User Interface — natural interactive dialogue tool.

Comparison: Information Systems Across Levels

System Type Target Level Primary Inputs Processing Primary Outputs
TPS Operational workers Raw transactions (orders, receipts) Sorting, listing, updating Detailed transaction documents
MIS Middle managers High-volume TPS transaction data Aggregation, comparison, summarization Summary & exception reports
DSS Senior/middle analysts Low-volume data, external models Interactive simulation, what-if modeling Optimal decision models
ESS Top executives Aggregate internal MIS/DSS + external trends Graphic summaries, drill-down slicing Strategic executive dashboards
ES Specialists/Clerks Domain facts & problem parameters Heuristic inference, rule-based deduction Expert advice, diagnostic solutions

5.7 Information Support for Functional Areas of Management

  • Interdependence — functional business areas are cross-linked; data generated in one department feeds operations in others (e.g., Sales Order $\to$ Production Schedule $\to$ Material Purchase Requisition $\to$ Finance Cash Outflow).
  • Core Functional Subsystems:
  • Marketing Information System — sales forecasting, promotional tracking, customer relationship management (CRM), pricing elasticity.
  • Manufacturing / Production Information System — master production schedules (MPS), material requirement planning (MRP), quality control inspection logs.
  • Financial & Accounting Information System — general ledger, accounts payable/receivable, cash flow projections, financial ratio analysis.
  • Human Resource Information System (HRIS) — employee skills inventory, payroll, benefits tracking, training records, performance appraisal histories.
  • Inventory & Purchasing Information System — stock levels, economic order quantities (EOQ), supplier lead times, purchase order tracking.

5.8 Organizing Information Systems

1. Centralized Information System

  • Structure — all hardware, databases, systems software, and IT personnel concentrated in a single central data center.
  • Advantages:
  • High corporate control and standardized operational protocols.
  • Easy maintenance, backup, and uniform software upgrades.
  • Superior enterprise-wide data security and access governance.
  • Disadvantages:
  • Extreme rigidity and slow responsiveness to localized branch needs.
  • Single point of system vulnerability; lack of customized local reporting.

2. Decentralized Information System

  • Structure — autonomous hardware, local databases, and dedicated IT teams deployed independently at individual branch sites/departments.
  • Advantages:
  • High operational autonomy and rapid responsiveness to localized branch requirements.
  • System tailored directly to unique departmental operating workflows.
  • Disadvantages:
  • Severe data redundancy and inter-unit communication silos.
  • Difficulty in sharing enterprise records; high aggregate maintenance and licensing costs.

⚡ Quick Recap

  • Data is raw input; Information is processed, contextualized output possessing decision-making value.
  • Good Information must meet 10 attributes: Timeliness, Accuracy, Relevance, Adequacy, Completeness, Explicitness, Impartiality, Validity, Reliability, and Consistency.
  • MIS provides structured reporting across planning, prediction, control, and decision support functions.
  • Information Hierarchy: Strategic (top / long-range summary), Tactical (middle / exception summaries), and Operational (lower / high-volume daily detail).
  • Information Systems Spectrum: TPS (daily transactions) $\to$ MIS (summary reports) $\to$ DSS (analytical what-if models) $\to$ ESS (top executive dashboards) $\to$ ES (AI rule-based expert deduction).
  • Functional Integration: Marketing, Finance, Production, HR, and Purchasing information systems operate in tight, interdependent feedback loops.
  • IS Architecture: Centralized provides standardized control and security; Decentralized provides local flexibility at higher total cost and risk of data silos.
